# Understanding the existing UI/UX of Rapido

Rapido UX is proof how app interfaces have to evolve alongside the business itself.

What started as a bike-taxi app has gradually expanded into autos, parcel delivery, metro tickets and travel. And when you look at different UX reviews and user research around Rapido, you can actually see this reflected in the interface.

Here's what Rapidos UX and UI has evolved into:-

#### <i> 1. Rapido understands the Indian commute </i>

One of Rapido’s strongest UX decisions has always been understanding the context in which Indians travel. Bike taxis solve a very specific problem: short-distance, congested-city travel where affordability and speed matter. After all, in a country like India, some routes are better gaped by bikes- Rapido observed this pattern! The ability to choose between a bike and a scooty, negotiate fares and even passengers rating reflects behaviours that feel distinctly local rather than simply copying global ride-hailing patterns. 

The UX therefore starts much before the screen design. The product is designed around the user's reality.

#### <i> 2. The home screen: simplicity vs discoverability </i>

Earlier versions of Rapido were criticised for putting several services behind menus, making features such as "Local Send Items" difficult to discover. Researching with users revealed that people struggled to locate the feature, understand what it meant, or know whether the service was available in their location or not. The problem wasn't necessarily the feature itself—it was information architecture and discoverability.

There were also concerns around:

> Important CTAs not being immediately visible
> Too much information inside menus
> Users taking time to find expected actions
> Features being hidden inside hamburger menus

This is a classic UX trade-off: More features ≠ better discoverability.

Rapido has since moved towards a clearer structure, separating major services into dedicated tabs such as Ride, Metro, Parcel, Travel and Profile. This makes the product’s expanding ecosystem easier to understand at a glance.

#### <i> 3. The booking experience: users want certainty </i>

One recurring issue identified in Rapido research is the waiting state after booking. Users could be left waiting for a captain without knowing whether someone would actually accept the ride. This creates uncertainty, which is different from simply having to wait.

Some suggested UX improvements included:

> Showing the number of nearby available captains
> Adding a countdown or estimated waiting window
> Making cancellation more intuitive
> Communicating low availability before the booking is confirmed

This is an important UX principle: Products should always provide certainty to lower the out of hand ambiguities.

#### <i> 4. Maps aren't just a UI element </i>

Rapido has faced its fair share of criticism around the map experience. Research pointed towards problems such as inaccurate location detection and the map resetting instead of remaining under the user's control. That sounds like a small interaction problem, but maps are actually a core interaction layer in mobility products.

Users constantly need to answer to their "current location", "drivers location" and "on road movement route location". So when the map behaves unpredictably, it directly affects trust in the entire ride experience.

#### <i> 5. Parcel delivery exposed Rapido's UX gaps </i>

The "Local Send Items" (now as "Parcel-Send Items") journey became particularly revealing on the UX gaps of Rapidos attempt at building parcel delivery.

Users struggled with:

> Understanding what the feature actually did
> Finding the feature
> Navigating multiple screens
> Seeing all required fields without scrolling
> Understanding unfamiliar icons
> Knowing why coupons appeared during payment
> Understanding service availability only after entering several details
> Knowing how to return to the normal ride-booking flow

Users were told “service not provided” only after completing several steps. That's a poor information hierarchy in the system knowing the constraint earlier—but communicating later. Good UX tries to surface constraints at the earliest useful touch-point, not at the end of the journey.

So, what does Rapido's UX tell us?

Looking across these different UX reviews, a pattern that emerges is that its strongest UI and UX decisions came from understanding context:  Indian commuters → short distances → affordability → congestion → multimodal travel → local behaviours.

Its weaker areas tend to appear when the product expands faster than its information architecture can adapt: More services → more menus → more flows → more edge cases → more cognitive load.

That's why Rapido's UX story is interesting to research and learn from. A product built from scratch, expanded crazy and is one of the faster evolving ride hailing app prefered over Ola and Uber of the worlds. 
Its simple- Rider first, user adaptive and affordable.
