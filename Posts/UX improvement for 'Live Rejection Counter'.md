# UX Improvement: Rethinking Rapido's Live Rejection Counter

*When transparency starts feeling like rejection.*

Imagine you're getting late for work. You book a Rapido, put your phone away for a few seconds, and come back to see:

**17 of 47 captains rejected your ride.**

That's an interesting product feature. But is it necessarily a good user experience?

I recently came across this feature on Rapido, and it got me thinking about how a seemingly harmless progress update can influence customer behaviour and perception.

## What is happening on the screen?

The screenshot captures an interesting part of Rapido's ride-booking journey.

While the user waits for a captain, the app displays:
- The number of captains who have rejected the booking.
- A progress indicator showing the matching process.
- An option to add ₹10, ₹20, ₹30 or ₹40 to improve acceptance.
- Alternative ride options such as Auto and Bike Direct.

From an operational perspective, this makes sense. The platform is showing that it is actively trying to find a captain.

But from a customer's perspective, the experience could feel very different.

## The UX problem: Transparency vs. perception

There is a difference between telling someone:

*We're still finding a captain for you.*

And telling them:

*17 people have already rejected your ride.*

The underlying information might be the same, but the emotional response isn't.

A customer booking a ride is usually looking for a quick solution, not an explanation of marketplace rejection patterns.

Seeing a rejection count can:
- Increase frustration during an already stressful wait.
- Create the impression that nobody wants to accept the ride.
- Make customers question whether their booking will ever succeed.
- Encourage users to pay extra just to improve their chances of getting a ride.

The last point is particularly interesting.

The app is introducing an additional payment decision at a moment when the customer is already waiting for a service they have requested.

## What could be improved?

I wouldn't necessarily remove the live counter. The idea of showing progress is useful. I'd rethink how that information is communicated.

**1. Show progress, not rejection**

Instead of highlighting how many captains rejected the ride, use more action-oriented messaging.

For example:

- "Searching more captains near you"
- "Expanding your search to find a quicker match"
- "High demand in your area. We're still looking!"

The objective is to keep the customer informed without making the experience feel discouraging.

**2. Make the next best action clearer**

The current screen offers multiple choices: add money, switch ride categories or continue waiting.

But it doesn't necessarily help the customer understand which option makes the most sense.

A better experience could prioritise options based on estimated waiting time and fare difference.

For example:

- Continue waiting for your current ride.
- Try an Auto with an estimated fare of ₹129.
- Choose Bike Direct at ₹71.

The customer should be able to make an informed decision rather than feeling pressured into one.

**3. Give users control over the experience**

If adding an amount improves captain acceptance, explain that clearly without making it feel mandatory.

The user should understand what the additional payment does and retain the choice to wait or explore alternatives.

## How would I measure whether this improvement works?

A UX change should not be judged only by whether users spend more time on the screen or accept an alternative ride.

I'd look at:

- **Ride conversion rate:** Percentage of initiated bookings that eventually get confirmed.
- **Search abandonment rate:** Number of users who leave during captain matching.
- **Cancellation rate:** Whether users cancel because of long waiting times.
- **Alternative ride conversion:** Whether clearer recommendations help users find another suitable ride.
- **Customer sentiment:** Whether users feel more informed and less frustrated during the matching process.

I'd also compare these metrics against the existing experience through an A/B test.

One important guardrail: improving conversion should not come at the cost of making customers feel pressured to pay more.

## My takeaway

What I find interesting about this feature is that the problem isn't necessarily the information being displayed. It's the way the information is presented at a particular moment in the customer journey.

A product can be transparent and still create anxiety.

As product managers, we often focus on whether a feature works functionally. But we should also ask whether it makes the user's situation better.

Sometimes, a small change in wording, the order of options or the way progress is communicated can make a meaningful difference.

**The goal shouldn't just be to show customers what's happening behind the scenes. It should be to make them feel that the product is helping them move forward.**
